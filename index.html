        What is a person worth at the register?

        Each person is scanned like a product. Their face is drawn in the barcode of their favorite thing, the barcode plays the music, and their favorite song only gets through the black bars. Unless the thing they love most has no barcode at all.

        1. 1The face is drawn in the bars: the darker the face, the thicker the bar.
        2. 2The 12 digits ring 12 notes as the scanner reaches them.
        3. 3The price sets the tempo: cheap things hurry, expensive things linger.
        4. 4The song plays over black bars and goes silent over white space.

        Getting the scanner ready…

        Demo cast: public-domain photos from Wikimedia Commons, public-domain songs performed by the site's synthesizer. Each barcode encodes the person's birth and death years; the favorite things and prices are imagined. To scan your own people, edit scanned/people.json (see the README).

        01

        About

        In painting, value means how light or dark a color is. Here it is also the material: brightness becomes loudness, and loudness becomes brightness.

        I built SYN-1 for ART 1901 (Studio Art for Non-Majors), Project 3: On Value. The prompt asked for a time-based work made of, about, or with value. Value as a concept, whether that's currency, hierarchy, time or dignity, and value as the medium.

        The project started with a question: if a painting is translated for someone who can't see it, or a song for someone who can't hear it, how much of its value survives the trip? Every method here keeps something and throws something away. Squeezing a painting into 36 rows loses its brushwork but keeps its composition; turning a song into mountains keeps its shape but loses its words.

        Because translation goes both ways, you can close the loop: play a painting, then paint the sound it made, and compare what came back.

        This is an art experiment, not an accessibility tool. Real audio description and captions do things a pixel-to-pitch mapping can't.

        02

        How it works

        Image → Sound

        1. 1
          Load an artwork of any size.
        2. 2
          Squint. It's shrunk to a grid, like squinting at a painting. Height is fixed because rows become pitches. Width keeps the proportions, so wide paintings play longer.
        3. 3
          Read. Every cell is measured as hue, saturation and value.
        4. 4
          Map. A method turns those numbers into notes, snapped to a musical scale.
        5. 5
          Play. Listen to a quick preview, or export MIDI and give it real instruments in GarageBand or BandLab.

        Sound → Image

        1. 1
          Load a song or recording.
        2. 2
          Listen. About 43 times a second, the engine measures loudness, which of the 12 notes are sounding, how bright the tone is, and where new hits land.
        3. 3
          Map. A method turns those measurements into shapes, strokes and colors. Notes become hues on Scriabin's color keyboard.
        4. 4
          Paint. Press play and watch each image follow the song.

        Under the hood: the translation engine is written in Python with NumPy and Pillow, and runs inside your browser through Pyodide (Python compiled to WebAssembly). The first visit downloads the engine (about 15 MB) and after that it's cached.
